Records, Traceability and Why They Matter

Traceability & Documentation

In most industries, missing paperwork is an inconvenience. In regulated and safety-critical work it can mean you are no longer able to prove a part is safe. Here is what good traceability looks like, and how to build it into daily practice.

4 min readQuality

01Why records are treated as a safety issue

In any regulated or safety-critical sector, from aerospace and rail to medical devices and nuclear, the same discipline applies: if you cannot prove with records what you made, what it was made from and what it was tested to, then for practical purposes you cannot prove it is safe. Good traceability is what turns "we always do it properly" into something you can actually demonstrate.

Nuclear puts this in its sharpest form, which is why it is worth understanding even if you do not supply the sector. The Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR) regulates UK nuclear licensees against a set of Licence Conditions. Licence Condition 6 (Documents, records, authorities and certificates) requires that the licensee "make adequate records to demonstrate compliance with any of the conditions attached to this licence", and that those records, once approved, are preserved for 30 years unless ONR agrees otherwise.

That obligation does not stop at the licensee's own gate. It flows down through the supply chain. If a component fails ten years after installation, someone needs to be able to trace exactly what it was, who made it, what it was tested to, and who signed it off. If that record does not exist, or cannot be found, the problem traces straight back to the supplier who made the part.

02What a requirement like LC6 means for a supplier

RequirementWhat it means for you as a supplier
Adequate records of complianceYou need to be able to show, with paperwork, that what you delivered met the specification, not just assert that it did
Long retention periodRecords cannot be treated as disposable once a job is invoiced and closed. In nuclear the reference period is 30 years, not months
Approval of record-keeping arrangementsFor the highest safety-significance work, your customer's own record-keeping arrangements, which your evidence feeds into, may themselves be subject to regulator approval
No unapproved changes to approved arrangementsIf a customer's documented process for handling your records is approved by the regulator, they cannot quietly change it without going back to that regulator
These obligations are checked in practice, not just on paper. Regulators carry out real, published compliance inspections against record-keeping conditions (LC6, and its close companion LC25 on operational records) at major licensed sites. The point for a supplier is simple: this is actively inspected, so the records your work depends on have to be real and findable.

03Traceability is your first defence against counterfeit parts

Traceability is not only about proving your own good work. It is the primary defence against counterfeit, fraudulent and suspect items (CFSI) getting into the supply chain. The nuclear industry defines CFSI as goods illegally labelled and represented as original, and warns that almost anything can be copied, including machined parts.

Why this mattersA counterfeit part can look identical to the genuine article from the outside while containing different materials, or skipping the testing the real part underwent. Knowing the genuine provenance of every part, material and record is the main practical defence, because it makes a substitution visible before the part is installed, not after something fails.

For engineering and design roles, that means defining technical specifications, critical characteristics and acceptance criteria as early as possible. For procurement, it means passing those requirements down through your own supply chain so they form part of goods acceptance, and, wherever practical, buying directly from the manufacturer rather than through unverified intermediaries. There is a companion guide, Spotting Counterfeit Parts, on recognising and responding to suspect items.

04What good traceability looks like in practice

  • A unique, unbroken reference linking raw material, in-process records, test and inspection results and the finished item, so any one of them leads you back to the others.
  • Records created at the point of work, not reconstructed afterwards from memory or a rough note.
  • A clear owner for record retention: someone actually responsible for making sure records exist for the full retention period, not just until the invoice is paid.
  • Certificates and authorities kept alongside the record they relate to, not in a separate system nobody remembers to check.
  • A documented process for what happens when a record is found to be missing or wrong, so you have a defined response, not just prevention.
A short worked exampleA fabricator delivered a batch on time, but a later query could not be answered: the material certificate named a heat number that did not appear on any of the cutting records. The trail had broken at one handover, where parts moved from the stores system into the job travellers without the heat number being carried across. The fix was not more paperwork but one added field on the job traveller and a goods-in check that the field is filled before material is released to the floor. The gap closed at the handover, which is almost always where these breaks happen.

05A realistic path to building this in

Stage 1: map your current record trailWalk one recent job from raw material through to delivery and see whether you could actually reconstruct full traceability today. Most gaps are found here, not invented.
Stage 2: fix the weakest link firstTraceability is only as strong as its weakest connection, usually the handover between two systems or two people, not any single record itself.
Stage 3: build a retention plan, not just a filing habitDecide, in writing, who owns record retention and for how long, rather than relying on records surviving by accident.
Stage 4: treat counterfeit-part awareness as part of the same systemTraceability and counterfeit-item awareness reinforce each other, so train the same people on both together rather than as separate topics.
Stage 5: ask your customer what they actually need to seeRequirements vary by grade and criticality, so confirm what is expected rather than assuming the highest bar always applies.

06Common mistakes to avoid

✗Treating traceability as paperwork to complete just before an audit, rather than a routine part of daily work.
✗Losing traceability at a handover point: between departments, between systems, or between your business and a sub-supplier.
✗Assuming retention obligations end when the contract does, when a 30-year preservation requirement runs long past project closeout.
✗Not knowing where records for a specific job actually are, even when they technically exist somewhere.
Does a condition like LC6 apply directly to my business, or only to the licensee?

It is a legal condition on the licensee, such as the site operator, but it can only be satisfied if the records your work depends on actually exist and are adequate, so the practical obligation flows down to you as a supplier.

How long do I really need to keep records for?

In nuclear the standard reference point is 30 years unless the regulator has approved a different period for the specific arrangement. Other sectors set their own periods, so treat your customer's stated requirement as the default.

How is this different from the Spotting Counterfeit Parts guide?

They are closely related. Traceability is the main practical defence against counterfeit items. This guide focuses on the record-keeping discipline itself; the Spotting Counterfeit Parts guide focuses on recognising and responding to suspect items.

Where does your record trail break?

Most gaps show up at handovers, between systems or between people. If you would like a second pair of eyes on how a job is traced from raw material to delivery, we are happy to walk through it with you.
