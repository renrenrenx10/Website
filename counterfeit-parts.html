Counterfeit, Fraudulent and Suspect Items

Spotting Counterfeit Parts

Counterfeit, fraudulent and suspect items can enter any complex supply chain. Here is what to watch for at purchasing and goods-in, and exactly what to do if you are suspicious.

3 min readSupply Chain

01What CFSI actually covers

CategoryMeaning
CounterfeitDeliberately made to look like a genuine, approved item, without authorisation
FraudulentMisrepresented origin, certification or test results, whether or not the physical item itself is genuine
SuspectNot confirmed genuine: insufficient evidence either way, and treated with caution until resolved

02Why this risk exists, and who it affects

Complex, multi-tier supply chains create opportunities for counterfeit or misrepresented items to enter the chain, sometimes unknown even to the immediate seller. This is not about assuming bad faith everywhere. It is about building routine checks that catch the rare cases before they cause harm.

It is also not a nuclear-only problem. Counterfeit and misrepresented parts turn up across every sector, from electronics and fasteners to bearings and raw material certificates. The warning signs and the response below apply wherever you buy parts or materials you have to rely on.

03Warning signs at the purchasing stage

  • Price significantly below market rate for the specification, with no clear explanation.
  • Vague or unverifiable supply chain history for the item.
  • Pressure to bypass normal procurement or verification steps to secure a deal quickly.
  • Documentation that looks inconsistent, incomplete, or recently altered.

04Warning signs at goods-in

  • Packaging, labelling or markings that do not quite match the expected standard or previous deliveries.
  • Physical inconsistencies in finish, weight or dimensions versus known-good parts you have received before.
  • Missing or incomplete certificates of conformity accompanying the delivery.
Warning signs are not proof. They are reasons to stop and check, not to make accusations. Most flagged items turn out to have an innocent explanation, and the process exists precisely to resolve that quickly and fairly.

05What to do if you are suspicious

StepAction
1. Stop and quarantineIsolate the item physically. Do not use it, and do not return it to the supplier yet.
2. ReportNotify your quality function promptly and follow your own reporting procedure. Check what your contract and your customer require you to report, to whom, and how quickly, as obligations vary by sector and contract.
3. Do not investigate aloneFormal investigations follow specific protocols and often need specialist input. This is not a task to handle informally.
4. Follow throughTrack the item and the outcome to closure, and feed the learning back into your purchasing and goods-in process.
Please note: the exact reporting route, including who must be told and how quickly, depends on your sector, your contract and current regulatory guidance. Confirm your own obligations with a suitably qualified colleague before relying on the steps above.
A short exampleA goods-in inspector noticed the laser marking on a batch of fittings sat slightly differently from the last delivery, and the certificate of conformity referenced a revision that did not match the drawing. The parts were quarantined with a hold tag, the quality manager and the customer were told the same day, and the supplier was asked to account for the discrepancy. The items turned out to be from a genuine but mislabelled batch. No harm done, and the goods-in check that caught it became a standard step.

What if I am wrong and the item turns out to be genuine?

That is a normal and expected outcome of a healthy process. Raising a concern that turns out unfounded is far preferable to missing a genuine one.

Do I need special training to spot counterfeit parts?

Basic awareness, like the warning signs above, is enough for most staff. Specialist investigation, if a concern is confirmed, is a separate step handled by trained personnel.

How does this relate to traceability?

Closely. Strong traceability is the main defence against counterfeit items getting in unnoticed. See the companion guide, Traceability & Documentation.

Is your goods-in process ready?

A good process catches a problem before the part reaches the shop floor. We can help you review your purchasing and goods receipt checks and put practical awareness training in front of your team.
